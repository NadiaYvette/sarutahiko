For **tone and voice**, the standard to cite depends strictly on whether you are writing industry technical documentation or an academic research paper. There is a sharp division in what authorities consider standard:

| Category | Widely-Recognized Authorities | Standard Tone & Voice | Key Style Elements |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Technical Documentation** | [Google Developer Documentation Style Guide](https://developers.google.com/style) [Microsoft Writing Style Guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/welcome/) | **Conversational, user-focused, and collaborative** | Second person ("you"), active voice, short sentences, zero filler words. |
| **Academic Papers** | IEEE, ACM, and the American Mathematical Society (AMS) | **Objective, formal, and text-centric** | Third person or inclusive first person ("we"), present tense for facts, high precision, strict avoidance of colloquialisms. |

## ---

**1\. Industry Technical Documentation Standards**

If you need a citable, widely recognized industry standard for software, computing, or technical manuals, two guides rule the tech space.

## **Google Developer Documentation Style Guide**

Google’s guide is widely cited by open-source projects and tech companies globally. \[1\]

> *   
> * **Voice**: Friendly, respectful, and approachable. Google advises sounding like a *"knowledgeable friend who understands what the developer wants to do."* \[2\]  
> * **Tone**: Conversational but not chatty; professional but not stuffy. It emphasizes writing for a global audience with clarity and explicit instruction. \[3, 4\]  
> * **Key Rule**: Use the **second person ("you")** and the **active voice**. Instead of writing *"The database is updated by the script,"* write *"The script updates the database."* \[3\]  
> * 

## **Microsoft Writing Style Guide**

Formerly the [*Microsoft Manual of Style*](https://www.google.com/search?q=microsoft+manual+of+style&kgmid=/m/0h424p), this is the longest-standing authority for tech writing. \[5\]

> *   
> * **Voice**: Warm, relaxed, crisp, clear, and ready to lend a hand.  
> * **Tone**: Less formal, grounded in real everyday conversations. It encourages using natural contractions (e.g., *it's*, *you'll*) to maintain a human element.  
> * **Key Rule**: **"Bigger ideas, fewer words."** Get to the point fast and eliminate fluff or weak writing. \[5, 6, 7\]  
> * 

## ---

**2\. Academic & Mathematical Sciences Standards**

For academic publishing (such as an ACM or IEEE transaction paper), the guidelines shift radically away from conversational tones. The authoritative guidance is found in publisher manuals and structural textbooks (like the [*Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences*](https://www.google.com/search?q=handbook+of+writing+for+the+mathematical+sciences&kgmid=/g/12bmxy6bz) by Nicholas J. Higham).

> *   
> * **Voice**: Objective and formal. Avoid personal opinions, emotional descriptors, and casual phrasing.  
> * **Tone**: Detached and evidence-based. While tech documentation relies on "you," academic papers favor the **inclusive first-person plural ("We show that...")** or the **third person ("The algorithm computes...")**.  
> * **Tense**: Use the **present tense** when describing a stable mathematical truth or an ongoing piece of code (e.g., *"Theorem 2 proves..."* or *"The framework handles concurrent streams by..."*). Use the past tense only when citing a historic, completed action by another researcher.  
> * **Key Rule**: Absolute precision. Never use colloquial terms or vague quantifiers (e.g., replace *"a lot of iterations"* with *"numerous iterations"* or *"an explicit threshold of \$N \> 100\$"*). \[8\]  
> *
