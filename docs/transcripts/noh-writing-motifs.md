Unlike **Kabuki** or **Bunraku**, which frequently rely on highly defined "stock characters" (like the tragic lovers writing suicide notes or evil samurais), **Noh theatre** handles character archetypes more broadly. There is no single "writer" or "calligrapher" stock character role. \[1, 2, 3, 4, 5\]

Instead, characters are categorized by spiritual or worldly classifications (such as *mae-shite* for a mortal form and *nochi-shite* for a ghost or deity). However, the act of **writing poetry, letters, or death notes** is a highly recurring motif performed by specific roles, backed by specialized theatrical terminology. \[6, 7\]

## ---

**1\. Notable Roles Who "Write" on Stage**

When writing occurs in Noh, it is heavily bound to the **warrior-poets** or **literary figures** of classical Japanese history (especially from *The Tale of the Heike* or Heian court history). \[7, 8, 9\]

> *   
> * **The Ghost of Tadanori (*Tadanori*):** In this famous play, the protagonist (*shite*) is the ghost of [Taira no Tadanori](https://www.google.com/search?q=taira+no+tadanori&kgmid=/m/05h59t), a real historical general and elite poet. The entire plot hinges on a poem he wrote on a strip of paper (*tanzaku*) found tucked into his armor after he was killed in battle. \[7\]  
> * **The Ghostly Courtly Women (*Izutsu*, [*Sotoba Komachi*](https://www.google.com/search?q=sotoba+komachi&kgmid=/m/0ywzg1x)):** Characters based on legendary female poets like [Ono no Komachi](https://www2.ntj.jac.go.jp/unesco/noh/en/play/characters.html) often re-enact or reflect heavily on the mental anguish of composing poetry. \[9, 10\]  
> * **Shamanistic Figures (*Utaura* / "Poem Divination"):** In the play *Utaura*, the protagonist is a male shaman who literally practices divination using **poems written on strips of paper** hung from a small bow. \[11\]

## **2\. Specialized Terminology Alluding to Writing**

Because Noh is minimalist, the act of physical writing is strictly stylized and governed by specific theatrical concepts: \[12\]

> *   
> * **Kogaki (小書 \- "Small Writing"):** While it sounds like it refers to penmanship, this is a vital technical term in Noh. *Kogaki* refers to **special variant performances** of a standard play. In a script book, these variations are literally noted in "small writing" next to the master text, indicating special choreography, altered chanting, or different mask usage. \[13\]  
> * **Kata (型) for Writing:** Noh actors rarely use realistic props like wet ink and actual paper. Instead, writing a poem is executed via a highly codified physical gesture (*kata*). The actor will typically hold their standard folding fan (*ōgi*) closed, using it abstractly to mimic a calligraphy brush (*fude*) sweeping gracefully through the air over an imaginary scroll. \[3, 12, 14, 15\]  
> * **Utai-bon (謡本 \- "Chant Books"):** This is the physical calligraphy text of the performance. Since Noh actors and the chorus (*jiutai*) do not improvise, the precise "writing" of the script is bound to these sacred, beautifully brushed vocal notation books used during training. \[16, 17\]
